Motion sickness on a catamaran: why it is not an obstacle to your first voyage

Category: Guides Author: Serenity Team Published: 6 August 2026 6 min read

Of all the questions people ask before their first time at sea, one leads by a wide margin: “What do I do if I get seasick?”

Most people picture cinematic scenes: a storm, huge waves, a deck tilted at 45 degrees and an exhausted crew hanging on to the ropes. Because of this stereotype, hundreds of people put off the dream of yachting for years and choose ordinary hotels instead.

In this article we break down how a holiday on a modern sailing catamaran actually works, why seasickness is the exception rather than the rule, and how to prepare so that you think about it for the last time while still on shore.

1. The physiology of motion sickness: why kinetosis happens

To stop fearing seasickness (its scientific name is kinetosis), it helps to understand the mechanism. It is not a pathology or a disease in the classic sense, but a temporary glitch in how the brain reconciles information from different senses.

On board, the brain receives conflicting signals from three systems:

  1. The vestibular system in the inner ear registers the body's movement and swaying.
  2. Vision sees stationary objects inside the cabin or saloon and sends the signal “we are standing still.”
  3. Muscles and joints feel the changing load underfoot.

When the vestibular data contradicts what the eyes see, the brain interprets this mismatch as a sign of poisoning and switches on a protective response — nausea.

The key point: kinetosis is caused not by movement itself, but by the conflict between the senses. Remove the conflict and the unpleasant sensations disappear. Every tip below is built on exactly this.

2. Design matters: a monohull yacht versus a catamaran

The type of vessel is the main factor in your comfort on the water, and it is precisely what explains most fears around yachting.

A classic single-hull sailing yacht has a deep ballast keel. Under sail it leans to one side — this is called heel. On an ordinary cruising passage the angle reaches 15–25 degrees, which is enough to send crockery sliding across the table and force you to move around the cabin holding on to the handrails. On top of that, a monohull is prone to rolling: it rocks from side to side even in a light swell.

A sailing catamaran is built differently:

  • Two hulls and a wide base. A catamaran rests on the water on two parallel hulls with a broad deck between them. It is the same difference as between a two-wheeled and a four-wheeled vehicle.
  • Almost no heel. Even in a fresh breeze a catamaran barely leans. Crockery stays level on the saloon table, and you can calmly walk the deck, cook lunch or drink coffee while under way.
  • Minimal rolling. Thanks to its wide footprint the catamaran does not rock from side to side. It moves smoothly across the surface — it feels closer to a train than to a boat.

That is exactly why we use only catamarans on our voyages: a level of comfort comparable to a villa that changes the view outside its window every day.

3. Route and weather: we don't fight the sea

A factor beginners overlook is the philosophy of sailing. In competitive yachting the crew's task is to get from point A to point B in any weather, strictly on schedule. On a cruising holiday the priority is different: the comfort of the people on board. From this come the three rules by which we build every route.

  • We sail in sheltered waters. We choose calm gulfs — the Saronic Gulf in Greece, the Aeolian archipelago near Sicily, the Amalfi Coast — where the sea is protected by islands and coastline, and the waves rarely exceed a few dozen centimetres.
  • We keep the route flexible. The skipper checks the forecast every day: wind, direction, wave height. If a swell is expected somewhere, we simply change the plan and head for a quiet bay on the leeward side of an island. The route is a guideline, not an obligation.
  • We make short crossings. No exhausting day-long runs in the open sea: our standard is up to four hours of sailing a day, and usually less. The rest of the time the catamaran is at anchor in a bay or moored by a coastal town.

4. How to adapt to the sea: what actually works

The period when the body gets used to the vessel's motion is what sailors call “getting your sea legs.” For most people it takes the first day, sometimes a little longer — this is individual and normal.

While under way:

  1. Stay in the fresh air. The best spot is the cockpit, the covered terrace at the stern, or the saloon with its panoramic windows. The flow of air on your face eases things by itself.
  2. Look at the horizon. The horizon is fixed. When your eyes see a stable line while the vestibular system feels motion, the brain builds the correct picture — and the conflict that started it all disappears.
  3. Don't sit in the cabin with your phone. Reading or scrolling in an enclosed space while moving is the fastest way to trigger seasickness.
  4. Take the helm. When you steer the vessel, the brain anticipates each of its movements, and the discomfort often retreats within minutes. Ask the captain — he will let anyone hold the wheel.

Food and water:

  • Don't go to sea on an empty stomach. An empty stomach handles the motion worse than a moderately full one.
  • Avoid heavy food before setting off. Greasy, very salty food and alcohol in the first hours of sailing only get in the way.
  • A light snack and water. Nuts, crackers, a banana, water with lemon. Many people find ginger or ginger tea helpful — the evidence base here is weak, but there is no harm in it, and the subjective relief is often noted.

5. The medicine kit: what really works

Modern remedies let even people who get carsick on a bus or a winding road cope with kinetosis. The key is knowing what to take and when.

  • Over-the-counter tablets (for example, dimenhydrinate-based) are first-generation antihistamines that act centrally. The crucial rule: take them an hour and a half to two hours before going to sea, not once the discomfort has already begun. As soon as nausea appears, the stomach almost stops absorbing the medicine and the tablet simply doesn't have time to work.
  • Scopolamine patches are applied behind the ear. This is a prescription remedy: a doctor prescribes it, and the patch must be applied in advance — usually 6–16 hours before departure, not right before it. Check the duration of action in the instructions for your specific product.
  • Acupressure bands apply pressure to the P6 point on the wrist. Many travellers say they help, although laboratory studies show no effect beyond placebo. The band is safe and has no contraindications, so it is worth a try — but we honestly don't recommend relying on it alone.

All our catamarans are equipped with an extended medicine kit, and the captain will suggest what is best to use.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you take medication regularly, have chronic conditions, are pregnant or are choosing a remedy for a child — consult a doctor.

6. And what if you do get seasick?

Most often people fear not the nausea itself, but the feeling that there is no way out of it: you are in the middle of the sea and have to endure until evening. In our format that is simply not the case.

We sail along the coastline and between islands, so the nearest sheltered bay is almost always 20–40 minutes away. If someone on board feels unwell, the skipper doesn't “push on to stick to the plan” — he changes course and drops anchor. At anchor the motion disappears completely, and within half an hour there is no trace of the discomfort.

Nobody is ever talked into “toughing it out.” This is a holiday, not a test of endurance, and the decision always comes down on the side of comfort.

7. Who should prepare more carefully

If you get noticeably carsick on a winding mountain road or on a bus, that is no reason to give up on the sea — just prepare in advance: bring a remedy that suits you, take it early, and spend the first day in the fresh air rather than in the cabin.

Children, pregnant travellers and people who take medication regularly should choose a remedy with a doctor before the trip. Write to us ahead of time — we will take it into account when planning the first days of the route and start with the calmest waters.

In summary

Seasickness is not a verdict and certainly not a reason to give up on sea voyages. A catamaran's stability, routes through sheltered waters, short crossings and a few simple rules do their job: over the past seasons only a handful of our guests reached for the tablets, and not a single voyage changed because of it.

The sea is made to be enjoyed: swimming in wild bays, greeting sunrises on deck and discovering islands you can't reach by land.
